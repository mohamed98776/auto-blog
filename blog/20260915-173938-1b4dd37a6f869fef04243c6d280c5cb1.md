<p class="isSelectedEnd">There have never been more ways to “do something” about aging. Botox starts earlier, collagen banking is everywhere, at home devices promise professional level results, and the line between prevention and simply doing too much can get blurry fast.</p>
<p class="isSelectedEnd">So we went back to <a href="https://www.instagram.com/dramirkaram/"><strong>Dr. Amir Karam</strong>,</a> one of the most recognized names in facial rejuvenation. The double board certified facial plastic surgeon has spent more than two decades studying how faces age, performed thousands of facial procedures, and built an audience of nearly one million on Instagram for his refreshingly direct approach to what actually works.</p>
<p>We’ve previously talked to him about <a href="https://thechalkboardmag.com/conversation-dr-amir-karam-aging-identity-2/">aging, identity, and what it means to still feel like yourself</a> and <a href="https://thechalkboardmag.com/future-proof-your-face/">how to future proof your face</a>. This time, we asked him to cut through the noise: What’s worth doing, what’s being oversold, and where can we all afford to do a little less?</p>
<h2 class="fancy-quote">So, What’s Actually Worth Doing to Your Face?</h2>
<h2><span class="lead-in">When someone says, “I just want to look younger,” what are the first things you look at before recommending any treatment?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
It always starts with improving the quality of their skin. Youthful, bright, glowing skin plays a huge role in the appearance of youth. This is always where I start.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">But it realistically depends on their age, of course. If they’re on the younger side, say in their early 40s and younger, improving their skin quality, a little bit of Botox, and possibly a touch of volume can do the trick. Once you’re in the late 40s and early 50s and structural laxity is present, a facelift would be in the near future to make a meaningful change.</span></p>
<p><img class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-183085" src="https://thechalkboardmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/dr-amir-karam-facial-aging-interview.webp" alt="Dr. Amir Karam in an interview about facial aging" width="720" height="500" /></p>
<h2><span class="lead-in">“Collagen banking” is being promoted earlier and earlier. Is there real evidence that starting lasers, RF microneedling, ultrasound, or injectables before visible aging appears changes how someone ages long term, or is much of this prejuvenation hype?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
As a concept, this does make a lot of sense. However, the particular application doesn’t have to be so complicated. Botox and injectables have nothing to do with collagen banking. The concept is that collagen production decreases year over year starting in our mid-20s.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The most effective way to address this is by reducing the breakdown of existing collagen with sun protection and by using skincare actives like retinol and vitamin C as some examples of topicals that help with collagen production and protection. To take it one step further, in-office treatments like regular microneedling can help stimulate collagen. The name of the game is to try to outpace collagen loss. With consistency, this can be achieved for the most part.</span></p>
<h2><span class="lead-in">Some patients find that Botox, Dysport, or Daxxify stops lasting as long or no longer produces the same effect after years of injections. When that happens, what should they actually do next? Should they change the dose or placement, switch products, take a break, or be evaluated for true treatment resistance?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
It’s very unlikely that the neuromodulators stopped working. I almost never see this. However, the expectations of what they can achieve as you age definitely change, and the distribution and dosage you use should also change with age.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When you’re younger, in your 30s and 40s, a little bit of Botox around your eyes and forehead can make you look significantly more refreshed. But if you use that same distribution when you’re in your 50s or 60s, oftentimes the change will be significantly less impressive because of the overall changes that are happening with laxity around the eyes, brows, etc. In fact, in many cases as you age, you want to avoid putting Botox in your forehead altogether to keep the brows from drifting down. So the tactical approach changes with age.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">However, if there’s truly a resistance that happens to a particular neuromodulator, for example, if Botox stops having any impact, then it is reasonable to try a different neuromodulator.</span></p>
<h2><span class="lead-in">From your perspective, which nonsurgical treatments produce a genuinely noticeable result, and which are marketed as far more transformative than they really are?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
My honest opinion is that there really isn’t a nonsurgical treatment that can meaningfully lift the face.</span></p>
<h2><span class="lead-in">At-home red light masks, microcurrent tools, gua sha, and facial devices are now treated almost like essentials. Which can meaningfully support the skin over time, and which mainly create a temporary effect that disappears within hours?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
The foundation to beautiful, youthful skin that improves year over year starts with sun protection and the use of active skincare ingredients such as retinol, vitamin C, niacinamide, ceramides, lipids, and hyaluronic acid. This is responsible for well over 90% of the changes you can ever hope to expect.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Only if you have covered these bases should you venture into at-home devices to give you a few extra percentage points of improvement. The best category here would be red light devices, in my opinion. They are safe and have some science to back them up.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I like microneedling as well, but I get nervous with people doing it at home because of the risk of infection if they don’t clean the needles correctly.</span></p>
<h2><span class="lead-in">Under-eye filler was once the go-to treatment for hollowness, but more patients are now turning to PRF as a more natural alternative. What is your take on that shift, and when does PRF make more sense than filler?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
PRF is not a volume-enhancing tool. It does help with improving skin quality, but it does not bring any meaningful volume back to the lower eyelids. For somebody who has moderate to severe volume loss, they’ll be very disappointed if they’re using PRF to help them with lower eyelid hollows.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For somebody whose primary concerns are crepiness and thinning of the skin, with perhaps an extremely mild appearance of hollows, they may see an overall improvement with this approach.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fat transfer and filler using HA are still the most reliable ways of addressing lower-eye hollowing.</span></p>

<h2><span class="lead-in">Let’s talk skincare. Doctor-founded skincare has become its own crowded category. You have been very clear that KaramMD was not created from a white-label formula. What formulation decision in KaramMD could only have come from spending two decades studying how faces and skin age instead of just a list of trending ingredients?</span></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;"><br />
I formed a deep understanding of how skin ages over the years. I’ve been treating skin from the day I started practicing. Combining different categories to address the key changes that were happening with age was a fundamental starting point.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I knew that everyone is losing collagen. The skin is getting thinner, leading to lines and wrinkles. I knew that pigment is increasing, leading to sunspots and discoloration. I knew the skin is becoming dehydrated and flaccid. I also knew that the skin barrier was changing for the worse as we age. Ultimately, aged skin would be characterized by fine lines and wrinkles, discoloration, dullness, roughness, and sensitivity.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">I’ve been actively addressing all of these regions, but with separate bottles for each ingredient. The results were fine, but patients were unable to remain consistent with these products because oftentimes the routines would be six to ten steps.</span></p>
